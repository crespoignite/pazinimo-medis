---
title: The biggest myths about getting ready for school
date: 2025-08-26
---
Every year before 1 September I watch parents' worry rise about whether their child is really ready for school. Parents of future first graders panic when they see their soon-to-be schoolchild no longer wants to learn, before the school year has even begun. Parents of future pre-primary children feel the first signs of worry, because they see their very childlike child and cannot imagine how they will become a first grader within a year.

For 95% of parents, getting ready for grade 1 is something very complicated. Ask parents to name exactly which knowledge and skills matter most for a future first grader, and they are often lost and cannot name the important things, or they focus only on academic knowledge. But is learning the grade 1 curriculum at the age of 5 or 6 really preparation for school? I will comment on the main myths I hear from parents.

## Myth 1: the most important preparation for school is academic knowledge

True or myth? Myth.

The Ministry of Education and Science website has the state pre-primary education programme. Read it. It sets out very clearly what a child should know and be able to do when they finish pre-primary. Academic abilities get up to 20% of the attention there.

More important is the child's readiness to learn, their emotional and social maturity and their executive functions: the ability to focus and hold attention, memory, self-regulation and not giving up after the first failure. Preparing for school does not mean learning the grade 1 course at the age of 5 or 6. It means building a basket of resources that will help a child learn and experience success in learning.

For a future first grader, concentrating and holding attention for 30 minutes is often a much bigger challenge than learning letters or reading. So is following rules and regulating yourself: raising your hand before speaking and not giving up after the first failure. Tidying up after a lesson and getting ready for the next one. Changing for PE on your own.

For a primary school child it is also very important to find friends in the class and be accepted among the children. To know how to make friends, to resolve the tensions that inevitably arise in a relationship and to respect other children's boundaries.

## Myth 2: a child must start school already able to write, read and count

True or myth? Myth.

**Reading.** The pre-primary programmes clearly state that a child should know some letters, show an interest in reading and want to try joining letters. But in the grade 1 programme the whole autumn is given to getting to know letters and learning to join them. Within a few months children learn to read. Learning to concentrate, hold attention and follow the teacher's instructions often takes much longer. And if you teach reading at home yourself, just do not say the letters by their proper names to the child, because then they never join up. For example, em-a-em-a does not make "mama". So we say them as we read them: m-a-m-a. In the same way, we do not say "el", "em", "en". We say "l", "m", "n" and so on.

**Counting.** Counting itself matters less (a child can learn it like a rhyme). What matters is understanding number: when you ask for 5 apples, the child counts out 5. So count how many crayons you took out of the house and how many you brought back. How much fruit is on the table and how much each family member will get. This should be part of natural life, not forced lessons. Just as important is preparing the brain for thinking operations, so the child has first skills in sorting and classifying. We learn this by tidying up every day: sorting toys and building blocks and working out which item belongs in which box.

**Writing.** If you really feel the call of a teacher, show your child how to write a printed letter, not a joined one. Every teacher has their own system, and relearning how to write letters is harder.

## Myth 3: if a child has not learned to read in the pre-primary group, it is the parents' duty to teach them at home over the summer. The primary teacher expects it

True or myth? Myth.

First, a child who is not pushed, not overtired and is physically and emotionally healthy is curious and eager to know. They will come to you themselves and ask what letter this is or how to read a first word. Then everything happens very naturally. Trust the evolutionary engine that drives every person, and humanity, forward. If the child has not come, it is not yet time. Children mature at different times, not only sexually but cognitively and emotionally. I have not yet seen a child of normal intelligence and without special needs who did not learn to read or write. It is only a question of time. The more we press a child, the more we risk demotivating them.

Second, do we really have the knowledge to teach a child? Does teaching give us joy? Are we called to it? Primary teachers do not spend 4 or 6 years studying primary-level mathematics. They study methods for "unlocking" every child and for teaching in an inclusive, interesting and creative way. They learn how to integrate subjects so children get a whole picture of the world.

Parents very often press children too hard with teaching because of their own worry, so I would check myself before rushing to teach. A test question helps. Ask your child: "Do you want me to teach you, to show you how to do it?" If the child does not at once shout "Yes, I do!", keep checking by saying: "But at school you will have a wonderful teacher who will teach you these things. Maybe it is not logical for me to teach you." If your child feels any tension about your efforts to teach them, you will see how after this phrase they feel relief and relax.

So leave the teaching of the child to the teachers and concentrate on how to develop, at home, the executive skills that matter most for a child to experience success in learning. I mean responsibility and a sense of duty; independence; the ability to hear something the first time, or at least the second; keeping rules and agreements; the ability to finish what you started; and respect for other people and their boundaries. Believe me, a teacher will value these skills far more.

## Myth 4: the earlier a child starts school, the better

True or myth? Myth.

The maturity of a six-year-old and a seven-year-old is very different. The older the child, the easier it is to concentrate, hold attention during a lesson and finish a task without being distracted.

Working memory differs. An older child can remember 4 or 5 units of information, a five-year-old only 2 or 3.

Older children have better emotional self-regulation. This is very important for concentrating on a task and coping with disappointment when something does not work out the first time, whether it is a project or a question of friendships. It also matters for waiting your turn, keeping rules, raising your hand before asking, and many other things that count at school.

It is obvious that a seven-year-old will find learning easier than a six-year-old. You may say: but perhaps the six-year-olds who worked harder will keep working harder later and overtake those who started later? No. Research shows the opposite trend. Children who start school from the age of 7 learn better, their achievement is better and they experience less stress. Their advantage lasts 2 to 3 years or longer compared with those who started school at 6 or younger. Long-term studies show that children who start school later more often reach higher levels of education. I would explain this by saying that learning brought them more joy.

And the other way round: children who start school earlier usually experience more stress. Learning gives them less joy because things are harder for them and they feel more "behind" older, more mature classmates.

One more thing. I always joke that when we finish school or university a year early, we take a year off childhood and add it to the child's working life, because nobody lets you retire any earlier. Do we really want to shorten childhood?
